    Opinion

    Lighter. Still ours. Nobody paid for these.

    Three questions the Coverage Matrix cannot answer, addressed here at insufficient length and with no methodology whatsoever. New for № 02; the first three are kept below.

    The instrument

    Why is the status page always the last to know?

    Because it is written by the people who most need it to say nothing is wrong.

    A status page is not a measurement. It is a press release with a colour scheme. Somebody has to decide that the thing is down, get that decision approved, and then type it. Every one of those steps happens after the outage, and each has a person in it who would prefer it were not true.

    The five events added to the Ledger this issue make the point five different ways. Optus took five days to explain itself. AWS needed a post-event summary to explain a DNS record that had been empty since midnight. Cloudflare's status page — hosted off Cloudflare precisely so this could not happen — went down anyway, by coincidence, and convinced the engineers they were under attack.

    None of this is scandal. It is what a status page is. If you want to know first, read an instrument. If you want to know officially, read the status page. Do not confuse the two on a Tuesday afternoon.

    The Editor · 2 min
    Security

    The most consequential routing attack of the decade needed no exploit

    The password was ripeadmin. It had been sitting in a public credential leak.

    The industry spent a decade building RPKI so that a stolen route would be rejected by every network that validates. It worked. On 3 January 2024 it rejected Orange España's own routes, on instruction from whoever logged in to the registry with a password a child would guess, and a fifth of the carrier's routes vanished for an afternoon.

    The lesson is not that RPKI is dangerous. Reject-invalid did exactly what it was told. The lesson is that the routing system now has a control plane with a login page, and the login page is the attack surface. Two-factor authentication on the registry account is cheaper than any tool in the matrix and more important than most of them.

    You do not need a BGP monitoring contract to be safe from this. You need a password manager and an adult in charge of the RIR account. Buy the contract as well — but do that first.

    The Editor · 2 min
    Geography, again

    We said the capital of the internet was a Virginia suburb. In October it proved it.

    us-east-1 is Loudoun County with a product name.

    Last issue we argued that the internet had ended up in Ashburn, and that the share-of-traffic figures quoted about the place were mostly economic-development marketing. On 20 October a DNS record in one region emptied itself and a good part of the consumer internet went with it. Nobody needed a marketing figure after that.

    The uncomfortable part for buyers is that most of the companies that went down had never made a decision about Northern Virginia. They bought a SaaS product which bought another SaaS product which was in us-east-1 because it is the default in the console. Dependency is not something you chose; it is something you inherited.

    The question for the matrix is not "which cloud" but "which region, three suppliers deep." The tools under question two will answer it, and almost nobody asks.

    The profession

    Are network people disturbed?

    Yes. Thank you for reading.

    The longer answer is that the field selects hard for a particular temperament. These are people who find a clean routing table emotionally satisfying, who name production routers after Norse gods and then appear genuinely surprised when Loki fails, and who hold views on cable management strong enough to end a friendship.

    The pager at 3am is not the tell — plenty of jobs have one. The tell is that they volunteer for it, and then recount the incident fondly a decade later, unprompted, at a wedding.

    Every other engineering discipline builds something a person can point at. Network engineers build the absence of a problem. The reward for flawless work is that nobody learns your name. You have to be assembled a certain way to sign up for that, and it is not entirely a well way. But the internet mostly works because of people constitutionally incapable of leaving a suboptimal path alone at midnight. Disturbed is doing load-bearing work in that sentence.

    The Editor · 2 min
    Geography

    The capital of the internet is somewhere you would never choose to go

    Not London. Not Tokyo. Not San Francisco. Loudoun County, Virginia.

    Amsterdam and Frankfurt have the stronger claim on romance — AMS-IX and DE-CIX are exchange points with genuine civic identity, the sort of infrastructure a city puts on a poster. Marseille has the better claim on drama, where the subsea cables come ashore and Europe's link to three continents arrives on one stretch of coast. Singapore has the better claim on strategy.

    Ashburn has none of these. It has office parks, arterial roads, and a data centre tax base that local government discusses with visible emotion. The share-of-traffic figures you see quoted about the place are mostly economic-development marketing and should be treated as such.

    But capitals are not about romance. They are about where the decisions physically land. The internet was supposed to be everywhere. It ended up in a Virginia commuter suburb.

    The Editor · 2 min
    The award

    The MVP is a university project running on a budget you would find insulting

    Not a vendor. Not a hyperscaler. RouteViews.

    Since the nineties it has quietly archived the global routing table, and essentially every serious outage post-mortem you have ever read leans on it. Every hijack reconstruction. Every "here is what actually happened at 14:07 UTC." Every topology paper. It is the flight recorder for the entire routing system.

    It is also run out of a university on funding that would embarrass a mid-sized marketing department. MVP awards normally go to whoever shipped the shiniest object. In this field it should go to whoever kept the boring thing running for three decades without being asked.

    Honourable mentions. PeeringDB, held together substantially by volunteers and trusted by everyone who has ever complained about it. And RIPE Atlas, which somehow persuaded thousands of people to plug a stranger's hardware into their home network for no reward at all.

    How we're funded

    We take money from vendors. Here is exactly what it buys.

    Independence is a claim until somebody shows the accounts. These are ours: what money buys here, what it cannot buy at any price, and what happens when a sponsor asks for more than they bought.

    What is paid for

    The masthead unit, section support lines, partner content, newsletter units, survey underwriting, and verified listings. Every one of them carries a label saying so, at the top of the unit, in the same size as the surrounding text.

    If you cannot tell whether something on this site was paid for, that is a fault. Report it and we will fix the labelling.

    What is not for sale

    Position in the Coverage Matrix. Access bands. The free-tier column. Which sources are listed at all. Entries in the Ledger, and how they are written.

    A verified listing lets a vendor confirm their own pricing and limits and adds a badge. It does not move the row and does not change the assessment.

    How we handle sponsors

    Sponsors see nothing before publication — not the charts, not the matrix, not the survey results. They are told their unit's placement and nothing about the editorial around it.
